Blockchain, Web3 & Distributed Ledger Tech

A blockchain is a distributed digital ledger that records transactions across a network of computers in cryptographically linked blocks, making recorded data very difficult to alter after the fact. Consensus mechanisms let a distributed network agree on the valid ledger state without a central authority, smart contracts add self-executing programmable logic, and the broader Web3 vision imagines a more decentralised internet built on these ideas — alongside practical governance uses like land records and certificate verification.

Key points

Distributed ledger, blocks, hashing, immutability
Each block bundles transactions with a cryptographic hash linking it to the previous block. Altering confirmed data would break this chain and is detectable, making the ledger practically tamper-evident ("immutable") once confirmed across the network.
Public vs private/permissioned
Public blockchains (like Bitcoin's) are open to anyone. Private/permissioned blockchains restrict participation/validation to a known, vetted set of participants — often preferred for government/enterprise use needing controlled access.
Consensus: PoW vs PoS
Proof of Work (PoW) requires miners to expend computational effort to validate blocks (energy-intensive). Proof of Stake (PoS) selects validators based on staked cryptocurrency collateral, generally far less energy-intensive.
Smart contracts
Self-executing code on the blockchain that automatically carries out predefined actions once conditions are met, reducing reliance on manual enforcement and some intermediaries.
Cryptocurrency vs CBDC
Cryptocurrencies (e.g., Bitcoin) are decentralised, non-sovereign digital currencies. A Central Bank Digital Currency (CBDC), like the RBI's Digital Rupee (e₹), is centrally issued digital legal tender backed by a nation's central bank.
Governance use cases & Web3
Tamper-evident ledgers suit land-record registries, supply-chain traceability, and certificate verification. "Web3" is the broader conceptual vision of a more decentralised internet, often built using blockchain-based technologies.

Example

A state government pilots a blockchain-based land-record registry:
  1. Property transfer records are written as transactions into blocks on
     a PERMISSIONED blockchain (only the land-registry department and
     authorised banks/notaries can validate new entries)
  2. Each new block references the hash of the previous block, so any
     attempt to quietly alter an old ownership record would change that
     block's hash and break the chain -- immediately visible to every
     participant holding a copy of the ledger
  3. A smart contract can be used to automatically release an escrow
     payment once both the buyer's payment and the seller's signed
     transfer are confirmed on-chain

Contrast with a cryptocurrency:
  - Bitcoin is a PUBLIC, permissionless blockchain with no government
    issuer -- anyone can transact or help validate
  - RBI's Digital Rupee (e-Rupee) is the opposite of a decentralised
    cryptocurrency: it is centrally issued, RBI-backed digital legal
    tender (a CBDC), not a blockchain-based cryptocurrency free of a
    central authority
Common exam traps

Don't confuse a cryptocurrency (decentralised, non-sovereign) with a CBDC like the Digital Rupee (centrally issued, sovereign-backed legal tender) — they sit at opposite ends of the centralisation spectrum. Proof of Work is energy-intensive because of computational puzzle-solving; Proof of Stake replaces that with staked economic collateral. "Immutable" does not mean data can never theoretically change — it means altering confirmed data is detectable and requires overpowering network consensus, which is impractical, not literally impossible. A public blockchain is not automatically the right choice for every government use case — permissioned blockchains are often preferred where controlled access to sensitive records is required.

Practice MCQs

Q1. A blockchain is best described as:

  1. A single centralised database controlled entirely by one organisation
  2. A distributed digital ledger that records transactions across a network of computers in linked 'blocks', designed so that recorded data is very difficult to alter after the fact
  3. A type of physical hardware storage device sold in retail stores
  4. A synonym for any ordinary spreadsheet
Show answer

Answer: B. A blockchain is fundamentally a distributed, append-only ledger shared across a network, not a single centrally controlled database.

Q2. Proof of Stake (PoS), an alternative consensus mechanism, differs from Proof of Work mainly in that:

  1. PoS requires no computers or software of any kind
  2. PoS and Proof of Work are exactly identical mechanisms
  3. PoS eliminates the need for a distributed network entirely
  4. PoS selects validators to propose/validate blocks based on the amount of cryptocurrency they 'stake' (lock up) as collateral, rather than requiring energy-intensive computational puzzle-solving
Show answer

Answer: D. PoS ties validation rights/incentives to staked economic collateral rather than to computational puzzle-solving power, generally making it far less energy-intensive than PoW.

Q3. A 'smart contract' on a blockchain platform is best described as:

  1. A traditional paper legal contract scanned into PDF format
  2. A contract that requires a lawyer to manually execute every clause
  3. Self-executing code stored on the blockchain that automatically carries out predefined actions (like a transfer of funds) when specified conditions are met
  4. A term with no technical meaning in blockchain systems
Show answer

Answer: C. Smart contracts are programmatic agreements that execute automatically on-chain once their coded conditions are satisfied, without needing manual intervention.

Q4. India's Digital Rupee (e₹), piloted by the Reserve Bank of India (RBI), is best classified as:

  1. A decentralised cryptocurrency with no involvement from the RBI
  2. A Central Bank Digital Currency (CBDC) — a digital form of the Indian Rupee issued and backed by the RBI, distinct from decentralised cryptocurrencies
  3. A private company's proprietary loyalty-points system
  4. A type of blockchain consensus mechanism
Show answer

Answer: B. The e₹ is RBI's official CBDC pilot — sovereign-backed digital currency, conceptually distinct from decentralised cryptocurrencies like Bitcoin.

Q5. The broader 'Web3' concept, at a conceptual level, envisions:

  1. A version of the internet accessible only through a single government-owned server
  2. A vision for a more decentralised internet, where ownership and control of data, digital assets, and platforms are distributed among users rather than concentrated in a few centralised intermediaries
  3. The complete elimination of the internet, replaced by an offline-only network
  4. A synonym for the original 1990s World Wide Web with no new characteristics
Show answer

Answer: B. Web3 broadly refers to a decentralisation-oriented vision of the internet, often built on blockchain-based technologies, contrasted with the centralised platforms of 'Web2'.
