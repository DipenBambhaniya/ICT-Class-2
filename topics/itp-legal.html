Legislative Framework for IT in India

Five Acts together give India's IT projects and digital platforms legal force: the IT Act 2000 (electronic records, digital signatures, e-contracts, intermediary due diligence — its cybercrime provisions are covered in depth in the Cyber Security unit, so the focus here is its civil/commercial role), the Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023, the Aadhaar Act 2016, the Copyright Act 1957's protection of software, and the Right to Information Act 2005's role in keeping e-governance accountable.

Key points

IT Act 2000 — civil/commercial provisions
Gives legal recognition to electronic records and digital signatures (so an e-document/e-signature can carry the same legal weight as a paper one), enables valid electronic contracts, sets intermediary due-diligence rules (an online platform generally isn't liable for user content if it follows due-diligence obligations), and establishes the Certifying Authority structure (headed by the Controller of Certifying Authorities, CCA) that issues digital signature certificates.
Digital Personal Data Protection (DPDP) Act 2023
India's comprehensive data-protection law: personal data may generally be processed only with the individual's consent (or another lawful basis), defines a Data Principal (the individual) and Data Fiduciary (the entity processing their data) with specific obligations on the fiduciary, and creates a Data Protection Board to handle enforcement and grievances.
Aadhaar Act 2016
Provides the legal basis for the Unique Identification (UID/Aadhaar) system and for using Aadhaar-based authentication for the targeted delivery of subsidies, benefits and services — it governs Aadhaar specifically, not personal data in general.
Copyright Act 1957
Protects computer software/source code as a "literary work", giving developers/companies exclusive rights over reproduction, distribution and adaptation of their code.
RTI Act 2005 — the accountability bridge
Gives citizens a legal right to request information from public authorities, reinforcing e-governance's transparency goals — many RTI requests are now themselves filed and tracked through e-governance portals, closing the loop between digital service delivery and public accountability.

Example

A fintech app builds an Aadhaar-based eKYC (electronic Know Your
Customer) onboarding flow for opening a savings account:

  1. IT Act 2000: the digitally signed application form and the
     app's electronic acknowledgment are legally recognized as valid
     electronic records — no paper copy is legally required.
  2. Aadhaar Act 2016: gives the legal basis for using Aadhaar-based
     authentication to verify the customer's identity for this
     "targeted delivery of a service" (opening the account).
  3. DPDP Act 2023: the app, as a Data Fiduciary, must get the
     customer's (the Data Principal's) consent before processing
     their personal data, and is accountable to the Data Protection
     Board if it mishandles that data.
  4. Copyright Act 1957: the app's own source code is protected as a
     literary work, stopping a competitor from simply copying it.
  5. RTI Act 2005: if this were a public-sector bank, a citizen could
     separately use RTI to ask how many such accounts were opened
     under a particular scheme — an accountability layer that sits
     outside the transaction itself.
Common exam traps

This unit's focus is the IT Act's civil/commercial role (electronic records, digital signatures, e-contracts, intermediary due diligence, Certifying Authorities) — its cybercrime sections (hacking, identity theft, data theft penalties) and the National Cyber Security Policy 2013 are covered in depth in the Cyber Security unit; don't re-derive them here. The DPDP Act 2023 is a general, sector-wide data-protection law — it is not limited to Aadhaar data, and it is a separate Act from (not a section of) the IT Act. The Aadhaar Act governs the Aadhaar/UID system specifically, including authentication for targeted benefit delivery; it does not, by itself, regulate all personal data processing — that's the DPDP Act's job. The RTI Act is about a citizen's right to information from public authorities — don't confuse it with a citizen's right to data protection, which is the DPDP Act's domain.

Practice MCQs

Q1. Among its civil/commercial provisions, the IT Act 2000 primarily:

  1. Only defines criminal penalties for hacking and identity theft, leaving civil/commercial transactions entirely outside its scope
  2. Gives legal recognition to electronic records and digital signatures, enables valid electronic contracts, and establishes a Certifying Authority structure for issuing digital signature certificates
  3. Regulates only physical paper documents and signatures, treating any electronic equivalent as legally void
  4. Applies exclusively to central government departments, with private companies and individual citizens left entirely outside its reach
Show answer

Answer: B. Beyond its cybercrime sections, the IT Act's foundational role is granting electronic records/signatures legal standing and enabling e-contracts.

Q2. Under the Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023, the entity that determines the purpose and means of processing an individual's personal data is called:

  1. The Data Principal
  2. The Data Fiduciary
  3. The Certifying Authority
  4. The Controller of Certifying Authorities
Show answer

Answer: B. The Data Fiduciary is the entity processing the data and bears obligations toward the Data Principal (the individual).

Q3. The Aadhaar Act 2016 primarily provides the legal basis for:

  1. General data protection covering every type of personal data processing across all sectors in India, a role that actually belongs to the DPDP Act
  2. The Unique Identification (UID/Aadhaar) system and using Aadhaar-based authentication for the targeted delivery of subsidies, benefits and services
  3. Software copyright protection, a matter that instead falls under the separate Copyright Act 1957
  4. Citizens' right to request information from public authorities, which is actually the subject of the RTI Act 2005
Show answer

Answer: B. The Aadhaar Act specifically governs the UID system and its use for targeted benefit delivery, not general data protection.

Q4. Under Indian law, computer software/source code is generally protected as:

  1. A patentable invention only, with copyright protection unavailable to source code under Indian law
  2. A "literary work" under the Copyright Act 1957, giving the creator exclusive rights over reproduction, distribution and adaptation
  3. Not protectable under any Indian statute, leaving developers with no legal recourse against copying
  4. Protectable only if the developer separately registers it under the unrelated Aadhaar Act
Show answer

Answer: B. Software is protected as a literary work under the Copyright Act, distinct from patent law.

Q5. Which best describes the RTI Act 2005's connection to e-governance?

  1. It has no connection to e-governance whatsoever, since it deals only with offline paper-based government records
  2. It gives citizens a legal right to request information from public authorities, reinforcing transparency, and RTI requests are increasingly filed/tracked through e-governance portals themselves
  3. It is simply another name for the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, just passed under an older title
  4. It only applies to information the government has already proactively published on the Open Government Data platform
Show answer

Answer: B. RTI reinforces e-governance accountability by giving citizens a formal right to information, and is itself often delivered through digital portals.
